A broadcaster publishes one high-bitrate 1080p60 H.264 stream to FrameWorks and restreams it to two
places: YouTube and Kick. YouTube takes the stream as it is. Kick documents a hard ceiling of 8 Mbps
for video, and this stream is above it. One input, two destinations with different rules.

Each restream now picks its video separately. YouTube gets the original video, untouched. Kick gets a
rendition that fits under its ceiling, taken from the renditions the stream already produces for
adaptive playback. Nothing is encoded a second time for either destination.

## Why passthrough matters

Every encode between the broadcaster and a destination adds delay, costs compute, and loses some
quality, because a lossy encoder working from already-compressed video cannot get back what the first
encode dropped. When the original video already meets a destination's rules, the best thing to send
is those exact bytes. For YouTube in this scenario that is what happens: the audience there sees what
the broadcaster's encoder produced.

The capped destination still needs something it will accept. A stream with a live video ladder is
already producing lower renditions so that viewers on slower connections can play it. Choosing one of
those for Kick means the restream costs no encode of its own. The decision is made per destination,
from the tracks the stream actually has and the limits that destination documents, when the restream
starts.

Audio is chosen on its own. A browser publishing over WebRTC sends Opus audio, and RTMP destinations
expect AAC. The restream can take the original video and a converted AAC track together, so a
browser-published stream can still go out with its video untouched.

## When a stream does not need a ladder

Some streams have no use for lower renditions, such as a stream that exists mainly to be restreamed
to destinations that accept the original. For those, the live video ladder can now be turned off per
stream. Audio conversion, thumbnails, and recording keep working, and the stream no longer produces
video renditions nobody watches.

The trade-off is direct. With the ladder off, there is no rendition to fall back on, so every
destination has to accept the original video. In the scenario above, Kick would have nothing it can
take. If you need to be sure which kind of video a destination receives, each restream target can also
be set to require the original or require a rendition, instead of choosing automatically.

## What it does not check

Destination limits are applied where the platform documents a hard ceiling. For YouTube, Twitch, and
custom RTMP destinations, the restream checks that the codec is accepted but does not enforce their
recommended bitrates, because those are recommendations rather than limits. Whether a publisher's
encoder keeps a constant bitrate or a fixed keyframe interval is not visible from the stream's track
information, so a destination that requires either still depends on how the encoder is configured.
And a destination can refuse a publish for reasons of its own.

The [multistreaming guide](/builders/multistreaming) lists each preset's limits, the per-target video
setting, and how to turn off the live video ladder for a stream.
